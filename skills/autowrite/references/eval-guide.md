# Eval Guide for Hiring Profiles

How to write the binary eval criteria that go into each company or role profile -- and how to recognize bad evals so you can re-research a profile that came back weak.

Adapted from the autoresearch eval guide. Same golden rule (binary, not scales). The application is different: instead of evaluating a skill's output, you're evaluating whether a resume passes a specific company's or specific role's hiring bar.

**Industry-agnostic by design.** autowrite is intended for any field where hiring decisions are made from a resume: software engineering, IT operations, construction, game development, design, healthcare, trades, sales, operations, science, education -- anywhere a resume is screened. The principles below are universal; the examples illustrate how those principles apply across several different industries.

---

## the golden rule

Every eval must be a yes/no question about the resume. Not a scale. Not a vibe check. Binary.

Why: Scales compound variability. Recruiter subagents are inconsistent on "rate the technical depth 1-7" -- they'll drift across runs. They are consistent on "Does the resume cite a specific project shipped in the last 18 months?" because the answer is either there or it isn't.

---

## what makes a good profile eval

Each eval should be a check the recruiter would mentally perform during a 30-second resume scan. It should be:

- **Binary.** Yes or no. No scales.
- **Specific.** "Demonstrates leadership" is too vague. "Resume describes at least one project where the candidate led a cross-functional group of 3+ people through a multi-quarter initiative" is testable.
- **Anchored to a real company or role signal.** The eval should map back to something the company / role actually screens for (drawn from job postings, company values pages, hiring manager statements, interview reports, professional standards in the field). Don't invent criteria.
- **Not so narrow that the resume can trivially game it.** "Resume contains the word 'leadership'" is gameable. "Resume describes a multi-quarter initiative the candidate led with named stakeholders and a measurable outcome" is not.
- **Verifiable from the resume alone.** An agent can't check the candidate's portfolio, GitHub, or licenses at scoring time -- it scores what's in the document. If a claim is checkable only via external proof, the eval should ask whether the resume *cites a verifiable artifact* (a URL, a certification number, a published reference), not whether the artifact is good.

## good evals vs bad evals -- examples across industries

The same binary-eval discipline applies regardless of field. Here are worked examples in six different industries to show the pattern.

### Software engineering (any subfield)

**Bad evals:**
- "Has technical experience?" (every resume in this pool passes; signal-free)
- "Is technically strong?" (subjective; not measurable from a doc)
- "Would do well in interviews?" (impossible to predict)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume describes a specific system the candidate built and shipped within the last 18 months with named technologies?"
- "Resume includes at least one quantified outcome (users served, latency reduction, throughput, deployments, time saved) rather than only activity descriptions?"
- "Resume avoids vague tech-cliches ('cutting-edge,' 'scalable,' 'industry-leading') in favor of specific systems and outcomes?"
- "Resume demonstrates ownership across more than one stack layer (e.g., backend + infrastructure, frontend + accessibility, or feature + deployment), not just specialist work in one narrow area?"

### Construction / skilled trades

**Bad evals:**
- "Knows the trade?" (too vague to evaluate)
- "Reliable worker?" (impossible to measure from a resume)
- "Years of experience seems good?" (subjective comparison)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume names at least one project of comparable scale (sq ft, budget, crew size, or duration) to the role's typical project?"
- "Resume cites specific certifications, licenses, or safety credentials with issuing authority and current validity?"
- "Resume describes work in the specific subtrade the role targets (e.g., commercial vs. residential, new construction vs. renovation, specific code jurisdiction)?"
- "Resume includes at least one specific outcome (under budget, ahead of schedule, zero safety incidents, named client) rather than only task descriptions?"
- "Resume demonstrates progression in role responsibility (apprentice -> journeyman -> foreman, or equivalent) rather than lateral movement only?"

### IT operations / systems administration

**Bad evals:**
- "Has IT experience?" (too broad)
- "Good with tools?" (vague)
- "Has worked at a big company?" (snobbery, not signal)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume names specific platforms / OS versions / tooling the role uses, with the candidate's level of responsibility on each (administered, deployed, designed, retired)?"
- "Resume describes at least one incident response or outage where the candidate was the named lead, with the resolution outcome quantified (downtime minutes, services restored, post-mortem authored)?"
- "Resume includes at least one infrastructure-as-code or automation artifact the candidate authored or owned, with a measurable adoption signal?"
- "Resume cites at least one current vendor certification (with year of issue) relevant to the role's technology stack?"

### Game development

**Bad evals:**
- "Has shipped a game?" (overly binary in a different way; favors title gatekeeping)
- "Knows engines?" (vague)
- "Industry experience?" (subjective)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume names at least one shipped title (any platform, any scale) with the candidate's specific contribution and shipped date?"
- "Resume describes contributions to a specific engine (Unreal, Unity, custom, proprietary) with measurable scope (features owned, systems authored, optimization wins)?"
- "Resume demonstrates work in the role's specific discipline (gameplay, graphics, tools, audio, AI, network, build) rather than only generalist credits?"
- "Resume includes at least one production-side outcome (frame rate target hit, build size reduction, content pipeline throughput, milestone shipped) rather than only feature descriptions?"
- "Resume references collaboration with adjacent disciplines named (designer, artist, producer, QA) rather than working in isolation?"

### Creative production (design, content, video)

**Bad evals:**
- "Has good taste?" (subjective, not scoreable)
- "Strong portfolio?" (the eval scores the resume, not the portfolio -- ask about citation)
- "Creative?" (vague)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume cites a publicly accessible portfolio URL?"
- "Resume names at least one project with a measurable engagement, reach, or revenue outcome (views, conversions, awards, attributable revenue) rather than only client/brand names?"
- "Resume describes work in the medium the role targets (print, digital, video, interactive, motion) with named tools and end-format deliverables?"
- "Resume demonstrates work at the role's scale (campaign vs. asset, brand system vs. one-off, series vs. single video) rather than scope-mismatched samples?"

### Operations / project management

**Bad evals:**
- "Strong PM?" (vague)
- "Has run projects?" (every PM resume passes)
- "Good with stakeholders?" (subjective)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume names at least one project with budget, duration, headcount, and outcome stated explicitly (rather than 'led various projects')?"
- "Resume describes a project that recovered from at least one named risk or scope change, with the recovery action and outcome stated?"
- "Resume cites the specific methodology and tooling the role family uses (Agile, Waterfall, Scrum, Jira, Asana, MS Project) with the candidate's role in each ceremony?"
- "Resume includes at least one cross-functional initiative with named partner functions (legal, finance, design, engineering) rather than working only within one function?"

### Director / VP / Head of function (technology, operations, product, or similar)

**Bad evals:**
- "Has leadership experience?" (every director-candidate resume passes; signal-free)
- "Strong manager?" (subjective; not measurable from a document)
- "Strategic thinker?" (vague; unverifiable from a resume)
- "Has worked at a senior level?" (too broad)

**Good evals:**
- "Resume states the candidate's directly-owned headcount at each director-level role with a specific integer (e.g., 'team of 18', '4 EMs and 14 ICs'), not vague scope ('a team', 'multiple engineers', 'the engineering org')?"
- "Resume describes at least one decision in the last 3 years to restructure, consolidate, build, or wind down a team or function -- naming what changed organizationally and the named outcome (not just the deliverable that came out of it)?"
- "Resume ties at least one leadership action to a business outcome at the level the company actually screens on (revenue, cost, time-to-market, retention, org velocity, customer acquisition) rather than only to an operational output (shipped feature, hit milestone)?"
- "Resume describes at least one person the candidate hired, developed, or promoted in the last 5 years -- with the specific outcome named (e.g., 'hired two seniors who became EMs within 18 months', 'promoted three ICs to staff'), not generic mentorship language?"
- "Resume describes a cross-functional initiative the candidate drove without owning all the functions -- naming the partner functions (Product, Research, Legal, GTM, Finance, etc.) and the specific outcome that resulted?"
- "Resume includes at least one situation in the candidate's history of navigating significant organizational constraint (reorg, headcount freeze, layoff, budget cut, strategic pivot) and names the decision made under that constraint?"
- "Resume names budget or resource ownership at a specific scale (dollar amount of opex, headcount budget, vendor spend at $X/year) rather than only describing what the team delivered?"
- "Resume surfaces at least one external-value differentiator -- something the candidate brings that an internally-promoted candidate at the target company would not have by default (cross-industry transition, scale shift, specific external practice brought in, public artifact like a talk / paper / OSS / podcast presence, board or advisory experience)?"
- "Resume demonstrates progression toward (or breadth at) director-level scope across multiple roles or companies, rather than a single director title that could read as titular?"

**Director eval anti-patterns to avoid:**

- **IC-shaped evals on a director profile.** "Resume describes a specific system the candidate built" is an IC eval. If the director profile contains mostly these, the recruiter subagent will score the candidate's org-scope bullets as failures (they don't describe shipped systems) and technical bullets as passes -- exactly backwards.
- **Title-gatekeeping.** "Has a Director title" is not a useful eval. Many strong director-level contributors held VP or "Head of" titles. Eval the scope, not the title.
- **Evals that collapse org scale into a pass/fail on a single dimension.** "Managed 10+ people" is gameable. "Resume describes both the team size AND at least one specific thing that changed because of how the team was organized" is not.
- **Evals that implicitly reward Highlight addition over Highlight substitution.** When an eval gap surfaces ("resume does not foreground X"), the recruiter subagent will often suggest "add a Highlight covering X." On a length-constrained director resume this is the wrong default. See the Highlights-management note below.

### Highlights management (director-level mutation rule)

Director-level resumes are length-constrained. Most land at 2 pages with a 6-bullet "Selected Highlights" section at the top. When a new Highlight is genuinely needed (typically because an eval gap surfaces that body bullets can't be skimmed for), the right move is **cut-before-add**, not append.

- **The cut candidate is anything whose proof also lives at body altitude.** If a Highlight's underlying claim is also stated in the Experience bullets, the Highlight is optional -- cutting it from skim altitude does not lose the claim, only its top-of-doc visibility.
- **The load-bearing Highlights are the ones whose claim has no body-level proof.** A Highlight stating a precedent or first-of-kind outcome (e.g., "established the first such function within the org") with no Experience-section restatement is load-bearing: cut it and the claim disappears entirely. Don't cut load-bearing Highlights to make room for net-new ones.
- **The rule applies per opening, not per candidate.** What's load-bearing for an AI lab is different from what's load-bearing for a healthcare org. The cut candidate is the Highlight whose claim is least relevant for *this opening*, not the Highlight that's least flattering to the candidate.
- **Reflected in the mutation loop:** when a per-experiment mutation would push the Highlights count above 6 (or above the candidate's stated maximum), the loop should prefer a swap mutation -- substitute the new bullet for a less-relevant existing one -- rather than an additive mutation. Append mutations on the Highlights section should be flagged as "Highlights overflow" in the changelog if they push past the configured maximum.

The framing is **spend Highlights on what THIS opening screens for, not on what's most flattering across all openings**. Across a multi-company autonomous run, this means per-company variants will have different Highlight sets even when the underlying body bullets are identical -- and that is the correct behavior.

## tier the evals by the JD's own lists

For a **hiring-manager (role) profile** built from a specific job description, tag each eval with the JD section it came from, and **report a tally per tier.** Do not collapse them into one aggregate.

Most JDs separate their requirements into three lists, and the lists are not equally binding:

| Tier | Typical JD heading | What it actually is |
|---|---|---|
| `minimum` | "What We're Looking For", "Requirements", "Minimum Qualifications" | The screening bar. This is what a recruiter filters on. |
| `responsibility` | "What You'll Do", "The Role" | The day-to-day job, and the interview-probe surface. **Not** resume-screen criteria. |
| `bonus` | "Bonus Points", "Preferred", "Nice to Have" | Explicitly optional by the company's own statement. |

Add the tier to the eval template:

```
EVAL [N]: [Short name]
Tier: minimum | responsibility | bonus
Question: [Yes/no question about the resume]
...
```

And report the score as three tallies plus the total:

```
minimum:        4 PASS / 1 PARTIAL / 1 FAIL
responsibility: 2 PASS / 2 PARTIAL / 3 FAIL
bonus:          1 PASS / 1 PARTIAL / 1 FAIL
-------------------------------------------
total:          7 PASS / 4 PARTIAL / 5 FAIL
```

**Why this matters more than it sounds like it should.** A flat aggregate silently weights an optional "bonus points" line the same as a hard minimum qualification, and the resulting number drives a go/no-go. In a real case the aggregate read `7 PASS / 4 PARTIAL / 5 FAIL` -- a bad fit, and the candidate dropped the role. Re-cut against the JD's own tiers, the six actual minimum qualifications scored `4 PASS / 1 PARTIAL / 1 FAIL`. Every alarming FAIL sat in responsibilities or bonus, and the seniority gap that looked disqualifying was literally printed under "Bonus Points." The tiered read reopened a real opportunity the aggregate had closed.

The distortion runs both directions. An aggregate can also look reassuring while the resume fails two hard minimums, because a stack of easy responsibility passes buries them.

**Consequences for the mutation loop:**

- **Mutate against `minimum` failures first.** A FAIL on a minimum qualification is a screen-out; a FAIL on a responsibility is an interview question. They are not worth the same mutation budget.
- **Don't spend Highlight space on `bonus` evals.** Skim altitude is scarce (see Highlights management above) and bonus criteria are optional by the company's own admission.
- **A `responsibility` FAIL is prep substrate, not a resume defect.** Route it to interview prep rather than trying to mutate a claim the candidate can't honestly make.
- **Report the tiers in the changelog and dashboard**, not just the total, so a human reading the run can see *where* the resume lands rather than only how high.

For **company profiles** (not derived from a single JD), tiering usually doesn't apply -- there's no authored list to segment by. Leave the tier off, or tag everything `minimum`.

## common mistakes when researching profiles

### 1. Too many evals (more than 12 for company profiles, 15 for hiring-manager profiles)

The recruiter subagent starts gaming them -- it scores high without the resume actually fitting the role. Like a student memorizing test answers.

**Fix:** Pick the 6-10 checks that matter most for *this company's screening process* (or 8-15 for a specific role). If everything passes those, the resume probably fits.

### 2. Generic across companies

If the same 8 evals appear in two different companies' profiles, you didn't research either. Each profile should have at least 3-4 evals that are clearly company-specific (referencing their stated values, their recent priorities, their interview reports, etc.).

**Fix:** When validating a profile, ask: "If I deleted the company name, could a reader still tell which company this is?" If not, re-research with explicit instructions to find company-specific signals.

### 3. Evals that test the *role family*, not the *company* or *role*

"Resume describes shipping a backend service" is true of every senior backend role anywhere. It doesn't help discriminate between Company A and Company B.

**Fix:** Tie evals to specific company priorities or specific JD requirements. For a company profile, this might mean referencing their recent strategic direction or a unique part of their culture. For a hiring-manager (role) profile, this means citing specific JD passages.

### 4. Unmeasurable by an agent

"Would the candidate fit our culture?" -- an agent can't reliably answer this from a resume.

**Fix:** Translate culture into observable signals. "Strong team culture" might decompose into: "Resume describes work in teams of 5+ with named stakeholders" and "Resume avoids exclusively solo-project language" -- both observable in a document.

### 5. Anti-resume evals

"Does the resume *not* contain X?" is sometimes valid (e.g., "Resume does not list expired credentials") but more often it's noise. Prefer positive framing.

**Fix:** Use anti-evals sparingly and only for genuine red flags (vague language, industry-specific cliches, unverifiable claims, expired credentials, etc.).

## the 4-question validation test

Before saving a profile, check each eval:

1. **Could two different recruiter subagents score the same resume and agree on this eval?** If not, it's too subjective. Tighten the language.
2. **Could a resume game this eval without actually being a better fit?** If yes, it's too narrow. Broaden the eval to capture the underlying capability.
3. **Does this eval map to something this specific company or role actually screens for?** If you can't justify it from a job posting, an engineering / industry blog, a hiring manager statement, or a professional-standards document, drop it.
4. **Could this eval also appear unchanged in 5 other companies' profiles in this field?** If yes, it's generic. Make it more specific to this company or role.

## template

Copy this for each eval inside a profile:

```
EVAL [N]: [Short name]
Tier: minimum | responsibility | bonus   (role profiles only -- omit for company profiles)
Question: [Yes/no question about the resume]
Pass: [What "yes" looks like -- one sentence, specific to this company or role]
Fail: [What triggers "no"]
Source: [Where this criterion comes from -- job posting URL, industry-standards doc, hiring manager quote, etc.]
```

Example:

```
EVAL 1: Project-scale evidence
Tier: minimum
Question: Does the resume describe at least one project of comparable scale to the role's typical engagement (budget, duration, team size, or output volume)?
Pass: At least one bullet names a project with two or more quantified scale dimensions (e.g., "$2M budget, 14-person crew, 9-month duration").
Fail: Only vague project descriptions; no quantified scale dimensions.
Source: <citation: job posting requirements section, company values page, or similar>
```
